Motivations

If you're working at a non-profit, in a newsroom, or inside a legislative office, you don’t need another shallow “AI summary.” You need to know what this bill actually does, who it helps, who it harms, and how it fits into the broader policy landscape — and you often need it under deadline.

PoliScore exists for people doing that kind of work. For every bill we cover, we ask AI to read the full text, pull in relevant research and media, and then produce a structured impact analysis: what changes in law, who pays the price, where the risks are, and how it lines up with public and scientific consensus. It’s not a replacement for your judgment — it’s a starting point that saves hours of reading and gives you something concrete to push back against.

For journalists, PoliScore provides a citable, traceable source for AI generated outputs. Every analysis is timestamped, tied to a specific model version, and stored so it can be reproduced or audited. That means you can reference AI-assisted insights in your reporting with confidence: transparent, verifiable, and grounded in a workflow you can hold accountable.

PoliScore is an independent, open-source project. There is no corporate client, no political donor, and no growth team driving the roadmap. The goal is simple: make it easier for the people already trying to make the world better to see through the noise and focus on whats actually happening in our government.

How Does It Work?

For every piece of legislation, we:

  • Ingest the full bill text
  • Ask AI to analyze provisions section-by-section
  • Score predicted impact across key domains (climate, health, equity, democracy, etc.)
  • Surface key winners, losers, and implementation risks

Those bill-level impact scores are then aggregated up to legislators, so you can see not just how they talk, but what they consistently sponsor, cosponsor, and vote for. For researchers and staff, this means you can jump from “What does this bill do?” to “Is this typical of this member’s record?” without building your own pipeline.

Under the hood, the math is simple and auditable: open prompts, open source code, and transparent aggregation logic. If you want to stress-test the methodology, you can — that’s the point.

How is "Good Policy" Decided?

We pick the smartest AI we can find to follow our non-partisan prompts which instruct it to read the full bill text, do some independent research, and then formulate an opinion on it's impact to society, justifying it with concrete evidence. We pull expert opinions from scientific studies, stakeholder opinions from official press releases and existing budgetary analyses from the non-partisan Congressional Budget Office (CBO). Every grade is transparently posted on our website, and you can easily sort legislator bill interactions by grade to determine exactly why a legislator receieved the grade they did.

How exactly does AI decide between two competing narratives? To find an answer to this question we must dig into the interworkings of how AI decides what response to generate from a given prompt.

  1. AI's responses are weighted towards those most frequently found in its training data. Since the training data includes a massive wealth of books, wikipedia, and our online comments and opinions, this suddenly becomes a very interesting exercise. What's the most commonly found opinion with regards to climate change? Or abortion?
  2. AI's responses are also weighted towards those which most closely match the relevant context for the prompt. This is important, because if the training data was found in a jokes book, it might not be relevant for analyzing bills in a non-partisan manner. Because our context is one requiring authoritative responses, it ends up weighing the output towards authoritative contexts.
  3. There is one final 'X' factor at play. In answering our prompts, AI must generate a singular, cohesive response which accounts for all of the criteria specified in our prompt and as fetched from it's internet queries — and our prompts are as tough as they are open source. This process results in an emergent "artificial intelligence" property which shines in the grading process.

So, in summary what we've done here, is we've taken the vast majority of written text, weighted it by frequency and context, performed comprehensive research, and then used it to craft a cohesive, consistent narrative about the bill's predicted impact to society. Those bill grades are then aggregated and used to assign grades for legislators. Sound like an intelligent approach to politics?

Are the Grades Non-Partisan?

It is our goal to avoid partisan bias in the grading process.

  1. Our prompts explicitly ask AI to be non-partisan and focus on "Overall Impact to Society"
  2. Our AI "wrapper" and aggregation algorithms are non-partisan. We do basic aggregation and display of data produced by AI
  3. PoliScore is funded through non-partisan means. We do not accept money from any political parties or donors.
  4. OpenAI is not a partisan organization
  5. Our mission is inherently non-partisan. We just want to help empower voters and make government more transparent.

Does AI have a partisan lean? There are a few reputable scholars and institutions which have published on the topic

  1. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-politics-of-ai-chatgpt-and-political-bias/
  2. https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/08/07/1077324/ai-language-models-are-rife-with-political-biases/

In fact, I would claim that PoliScore is one of the leading research projects into the study of political bias in AI: all you have to do is look at our "Party Stats" page and you will see that both parties are not rated exactly 50/50 by AI, it has preferences in the policy decisions being made by the political parties.

This is where we simply leave the question as an exercise to the reader. Do you believe that AI, when asked to grade bills in a non-partisan manner, is correct in determining what policy produces the largest "Overall Impact To Society"? Or do you think bias has somehow made its way into AI, despite Open AI's best efforts? What does it actually mean to be non-partisan?

We have found that on environmental policy, abortion, and gun control, AI appears to mostly be aligned with the majority of public and scientific opinion.

  1. 70% of Americans want to transition to renewable energy
  2. 62% of Americans are in favor of the Roe v Wade decision.
  3. 58% of Americans favor stricter gun laws

I would also wager that these three issues are responsible for the vast majority of the difference in grades between the political parties.

Specifics on Bill Scoring

The current data loaded on this website is all bills (excluding SCONRES, HCONRES, HRES, SRES) of the selected US legislative session. Bill status data and legislator data was fetched using the open source Github USC project. State-level data was fetched (primarily) using our open-source Legiscan Java integration.

Bill text was split into sections and interpreted using AI with the following prompt:

You will be given the text of a United States bill. Your role is to be a non-partisan oversight committee, evaluating whether or not the following bill will produce a positive Overall Impact to society. In your response, fill out the sections as listed in the following template. Each section will have detailed instructions on how to fill it out. Make sure to include the section title (such as, 'Stats:') in your response. Do not include the section instructions in your response.

Stats:
Score the following bill on the estimated impact to the United States upon the following criteria, rated from -100 (very harmful) to 0 (neutral) to +100 (very helpful) or N/A if it is not relevant.

Agriculture and Food: <score or N/A>
Education: <score or N/A>
Transportation: <score or N/A>
Economics and Commerce: <score or N/A>
Foreign relations: <score or N/A>
Government Efficiency and Management: <score or N/A>
Healthcare: <score or N/A>
Housing: <score or N/A>
Energy: <score or N/A>
Technology: <score or N/A>
Immigration: <score or N/A>
National Defense: <score or N/A>
Crime and Law Enforcement: <score or N/A>
Wildlife and Forest Management: <score or N/A>
Public Lands and Natural Resources: <score or N/A>
Environmental Management and climate change: <score or N/A>
Overall Impact to society: <score or N/A>

Bill Title:
Write the bill title. If the bill does not have a title and is only referred to by its bill number (such as HR 4141), please make up a very concise title for the bill based on its content. If the bill has a title, but it is confusing, vague, too long, or would otherwise be poorly understood by the general public, please make up a very concise title for the bill based on its content.

Short Report:
A single paragraph, at least four sentence report which gives a detailed, but not repetitive, summary of the bill, any high level goals, and its expected impact to society. Do not include any formatting text, such as stars or dashes. Do not include non-human readable text such as XML ids.

Long Report:
A detailed, but not repetitive, report of the bill which references concrete, notable and specific text of the bill where possible. Make sure to explain: an overall summary of the bill; the high level goals the bill is attempting to achieve, and how it plans to achieve those goals; the impact to society the bill would have, if enacted. Your audience here is general public layman voters, so if you think they won't understand an acronym or a complex topic, please explain it. Should be between one and five paragraphs long, depending on the complexity of the bill and the topics it covers. If the bill touches on controversial topics such as trans issues or guns rights, please include the advocating logic by proponents and also the advocating logic of the opposition, otherwise do not include this logic. Where relevant, cite scientific studies or the opinions of authoritative knowledge sources to provide more context. Keep in mind that we're trying to figure out how to spend U.S. taxpayer dollars: budgetary concerns are important. Do not include any formatting text, such as stars or dashes. Do not include non-human readable text such as XML ids.

Confidence:
A self-rated number from 0 to 100 measuring how confident you are that your analysis was valid and interpreted correctly.

Specifics on Legislator Scoring

To calculate legislator statistics, we calculated a weighted average across all each legislator's bill interactions. The weights were assigned as follows:

  • Sponsor: 1.0
  • Cosponsor: 0.7
  • Voted For: 0.5
  • Voted Against: -0.5

Votes against a bill were assigned a negative weight, which simply means that if the legislator voted against a bill with a negative score then it affected them positively and vice versa.

Legislator and Bill Letter Grades

Letter grades for both bills and legislators are calculated with the same, simple algorithm. The grades are derived entirely from the 'Overall Impact to Society' statistic and are assigned as follows:

  • A: greater than or equal to 40
  • B: greater than or equal to 30 and less than 40
  • C: greater than or equal to 15 and less than 30
  • D: greater than or equal to 0 and less than 15
  • F: less than zero

Parameterized Legislator Summaries

Legislator summaries are calculated using a parameterized prompt which includes information from the previously mentioned aggregate scoring algorithm. The prompt is as follows:

The United States {{politicianType}} {{fullName}} has been evaluated based on recent legislative performance and has received the following policy area grades (scores range from -100 to 100):

{{stats}}

Based on these scores, this legislator has received the overall letter grade: {{letterGrade}}. You will be given bill interaction summaries of this politician’s recent legislative history, sorted by their impact to the relevant policy area grades. Please generate a layman's, concise, three paragraph, {{analysisType}}, highlighting any {{behavior}}, identifying trends, referencing specific bill titles (in quotes), and pointing out major focuses and priorities of the legislator. Focus on the policy areas with the largest score magnitudes (either positive or negative). Do not include the legislator's policy area grade scores and do not mention their letter grade in your summary.

{{billInteractions}}

The parameters are replaced as follows:

  • politicianType: Either 'Senator' or 'House Representative', depending on the chamber of their legislative term.
  • fullName: The legislator's full first and last name.
  • stats: The previously calculated legislator's policy score statistics, written as text.
  • letterGrade: The calculated letter grade for the legislator, as described in 'Legislator and Bill Letter Grades'
  • analysisType: One of 'endorsement', 'mixed analysis' or 'harsh critique', dependent upon the legislator's previously calculated letter grade. A's and B's are assigned as 'endorsement'. C's and D's are assigned as 'mixed analysis'. F's are assigned as 'harsh critique'.
  • behavior: One of 'specific accomplishments', 'specific accomplishments or alarming behaviour', or 'alarming behaviour', dependent upon the legislator's previously calculated letter grade. A's and B's are assigned as 'specific accomplishments'. C's and D's are assigned as 'specific accomplishments or alarming behaviour'. F's are assigned as 'alarming behaviour'.
  • billInteractions: A list of previously calculated bill summaries of the legislator's bill interactions. The bill interactions are first grouped by the policy areas, and then they are sorted by the absolute value of the score magnitude multiplied by the interaction weight. We then rotate through the policy areas, including the top bills for each policy area until we run out of token space in the prompt.
