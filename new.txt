Bill Title:
Telecom Cybersecurity Transparency Act

Short Report:
The Telecom Cybersecurity Transparency Act compels the Department of Homeland Security to publish, in full, an unclassified 2022 report that details major security weaknesses in U.S. telephone networks. Supporters argue that CISA’s decision to keep the study “For Official Use Only” hid critical warnings that might have prevented the 2024 Salt Typhoon hacks and other intrusions. Because the report is already unclassified, releasing it poses little genuine risk while arming Congress, regulators, and the public with the data needed to demand stronger standards from carriers. The bill imposes virtually no cost and modestly improves national security and governmental accountability, yielding a net positive—though meaningful benefits will depend on follow-up action once the findings are public.

Long Report:
The bill’s sole purpose is to require DHS to make public an unclassified study—“U.S. Telecommunications Insecurity 2022”—within 30 days. Senator Ron Wyden, who has read the document under restricted access, states that it contains “frankly shocking” evidence of vulnerabilities in the SS7 and Diameter signalling protocols that route calls and texts across U.S. carriers, weaknesses foreign hackers later exploited in the high-profile Salt Typhoon breaches of 2024 - 25. ([wyden.senate.gov](https://www.wyden.senate.gov/news/press-releases/wyden-calls-for-release-of-unclassified-report-on-national-security-threats-to-us-phone-system)) By transforming a discretionary “For Official Use Only” label into a statutory release mandate, the act sidesteps bureaucratic delay and FOIA lawsuits, giving lawmakers, regulators, academics, and consumers the raw facts needed to craft or demand better security rules.

Transparency has practical value. The FCC has already invoked Salt Typhoon to issue emergency orders requiring carriers to certify cybersecurity programs. ([bleepingcomputer.com](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/fcc-orders-telecoms-to-secure-their-networks-after-salt-tyhpoon-hacks/)) Public release of the underlying technical findings should help the FCC refine those rules, aid state regulators, and let independent researchers verify carrier compliance. Foreign policy experts also note that exposing how adversaries leveraged U.S. telecom infrastructure supports diplomatic efforts to pressure offending nations. ([washingtonpost.com](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2025/07/16/china-hacking-us-targets/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The bill’s fiscal footprint is minimal—essentially the cost of uploading a PDF and perhaps redacting personal identifiers that FOIA would already protect. That near-zero expense contrasts with the billions companies and the government already spend responding to hacks. Even a small reduction in future breach likelihood would outweigh implementation costs.

Risks are limited. Because the report is unclassified, DHS has already judged that it contains no information whose release would damage national security. Critics may argue that disclosing specifics could aid copy-cat attackers, but after two years of withheld circulation the same data is almost certainly available to sophisticated threat actors; withholding mainly deprives defenders and voters.

Winners therefore include consumers whose data ride on these networks, policymakers hungry for evidence-based oversight, and smaller carriers that have invested in stronger security and will benefit from a level regulatory playing field. Large incumbents could face reputational harm and new compliance costs once deficiencies are laid bare, and DHS/CISA leadership may come under scrutiny for delaying the release. Nonetheless, the broader societal gain from improved cyber-resilience and governmental accountability outweighs these downsides, meriting a modestly positive overall grade.
